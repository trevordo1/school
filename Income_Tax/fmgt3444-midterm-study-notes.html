1 · Tax system fundamentals

Why tax? Who collects what? M1

  • Main purpose of taxation: to pay for government programs and public services such as health care, education, infrastructure, public safety and social programs. A second purpose is to influence behaviour (saving, working, investing).
  • Main tax types:
    • Income tax: collected by the federal government and the provinces.
    • Sales tax: GST/PST/HST.
    • Excise tax: on alcohol, tobacco, fuel and similar goods.
    • Property tax: on land and buildings. Municipalities mainly collect property tax.
  • The federal Income Tax Act is the main legislation. The CRA administers federal tax and the personal tax of every province and territory except Quebec (Revenu Québec handles Quebec).

Progressive taxation M1, M2

Income is split into brackets. Each bracket's rate applies only to the income inside that bracket. Moving into a higher bracket does not tax all of your income at the higher rate.

RateDefinitionFormula / use
MarginalThe rate on the next (or last) dollar earnedUse it to value deductions and to find the tax on extra income
AverageThe overall tax burdenTotal income tax ÷ total income
Effective marginal (EMTR)The marginal rate plus income-tested benefits or credits lost when you earn one more dollar(Extra tax + benefits lost) ÷ extra income
Worked example (M1)
Brackets: 15% on first $50,000; 25% on the next band Income $60,000 $50,000 × 15% = $7,500 $10,000 × 25% = $2,500 Total tax = $10,000 Marginal rate = 25% Average rate = $10,000 ÷ $60,000 = 16.67%
M2 version: $80,000 of income → $7,500 + ($30,000 × 25%) = $15,000 of tax. The average rate is 18.75%. An extra $1,000 of income costs $250 because it stays in the 25% bracket.
EMTR example (illustrative)
Earn an extra $1,000 at a 30% marginal rate → $300 more tax That same $1,000 reduces an income-tested benefit by $150 EMTR = ($300 + $150) ÷ $1,000 = 45% (higher than the 30% bracket)
MC trapsThe tax bracket a person is "in" is their marginal rate, not their average rate. The average rate is always at or below the top marginal rate. The EMTR can be higher than the stated bracket rate.

Tax planning, avoidance, evasion and GAAR M1

ConceptLegal?Example
Tax planning: arranging your affairs within the lawLegalContributing to an RRSP
Tax avoidance: using legal planning to gain a tax advantageTechnically legal, but can be denied if it abuses the lawA contrived series of transactions with little commercial purpose
Tax evasion: deliberately breaking the law by hiding income, inventing expenses or giving false informationIllegalNot reporting cash income; fake receipts
GAAR (General Anti-Avoidance Rule)A CRA toolCan deny a tax benefit from a transaction that follows the wording of the law but abuses its purpose ("object and spirit")
One-line distinctionAvoidance follows the letter of the law, and GAAR can catch it when it abuses the law's purpose. Evasion breaks the law outright.

2 · Filing and CRA administration

Deadlines dates: hint sheet concept: M2

WhoFiling deadlineBalance owing due
Most individualsApril 30April 30
Self-employed individuals and their spouses or partnersJune 15Still April 30
Classic trap (M2 "Priya" case)Filing and paying are separate obligations. A self-employed person who files on June 15 but pays on June 15 is charged interest from May 1. A later filing date does not extend the payment date.

Canada uses self-assessment M2. Taxpayers report their income, claim deductions and credits, calculate their tax and keep records. Tax withheld at source is a prepayment, not the final liability.

Why file even when no tax is owed M6 list: hint sheet

Filing a return can establish or keep:

  • Refunds: for example, when tax was withheld and isn't owed.
  • Benefits and refundable credits: GST/HST credit, Canada Child Benefit, and others. These are based on the return.
  • Credits: for example, tuition credits carried forward.
  • RRSP contribution room: earned income must be reported to generate room. It appears on the NOA.
  • Loss balances: for example, net capital losses carried forward.

Review vs audit vs assessment vs reassessment M2 audit & 3 yrs

TermWhat it is
AssessmentThe CRA's first processing of the return and its determination of tax, a refund or a balance owing. It results in a Notice of Assessment (NOA).
ReviewA routine check of specific claims, often a request for receipts or a matching of slips. It can happen before or after assessment. It is not an accusation of wrongdoing (M2).
AuditA deeper, broader examination of a taxpayer's books, records and returns. It is usually risk-based and may lead to a reassessment.
ReassessmentThe CRA changes an earlier assessment and issues a Notice of Reassessment. This can follow a review, an audit or the taxpayer's own adjustment request.
  • Normal reassessment period: for most individuals, generally 3 years from the date of the original NOA. There's no time limit when there was misrepresentation from carelessness, neglect or wilful default, or fraud.
  • Correcting an omitted amount (a missed slip or a forgotten receipt): request a return adjustment, through "Change my return" in CRA My Account, ReFILE or Form T1-ADJ. Don't file a second return.
  • Adjustment vs objection (M2): an adjustment corrects your own return. A notice of objection formally disputes the CRA's assessment. If you agree that you missed something, request an adjustment.

4 · Deductions, credits and expenses

Deduction vs credit: the most-tested idea M1, M5

DeductionTax credit
What it reducesIncome subject to tax (taxable income)Tax payable directly
ValueAmount × marginal tax rate, so it's worth more to high earnersAmount × credit rate (federal base rate 15%), the same for everyone
ExamplesRRSP contributions · qualifying childcare · moving expenses · employment expenses · deductible spousal supportCharitable donations · tuition · eligible political contributions · basic personal amount
M5 slide example
$1,000 RRSP deduction at 30% marginal → saves $300 $1,000 tuition credit at 15% → saves $150
  • Non-refundable credit: can reduce tax payable to zero but not below zero, so it can't create a refund on its own. Unused tuition can be carried forward. Examples: tuition, donations, BPA.
  • Refundable credit (for contrast): can create a refund even when no tax is owed. Example: the GST/HST credit.

Credits on the hint sheet

Spousal support conditions:

  1. It's paid under a written agreement or court order.
  2. The payments are periodic (a lump sum generally doesn't qualify).
  3. The spouses are living separate and apart.

Not deductible M3 hint sheet

  • Personal expenses, such as clothing, groceries and personal phone use.
  • Ordinary commuting between home and your regular workplace. This is personal travel, even though you have to get to work (M3).

Home-office expenses Hint sheet only

The workspace must meet one of these two tests:

  1. Principal place of work: the space is where the employee mainly works (more than 50% of the time over at least 4 consecutive weeks), or
  2. The space is used exclusively to earn employment income and used on a regular and continuous basis to meet clients or others.

Employees also need a signed T2200, and they claim the expenses on the T777.

5 · Investment and family tax planning

Interest income M4

  • Interest is fully included in income and taxed at your marginal rate. It gets no special treatment.
  • It must be reported even when no slip is issued. Multi-year investments may require annual accrual, which means you pay tax before you receive the cash.
  • Principal returned at maturity is not income.
Example (M4, Lauro)
$5,000 GIC at 4.5% → receives $5,225 Interest income = $225 (the $5,000 principal is not income) Tax at 40% marginal = $225 × 40% = $90

Capital gains and losses M4

Capital gain (loss) = Proceeds − ACB − selling costs Taxable capital gain = Capital gain × inclusion rate (50%) Allowable capital loss = Capital loss × inclusion rate
Key ruleAllowable capital losses offset taxable capital gains only, not employment or other income. Unused net capital losses carry back 3 years and forward indefinitely. Report them on Schedule 3.
Example (M4)
Proceeds $42,000 − ACB $30,000 − commission $500 = $11,500 capital gain Taxable capital gain = $11,500 × 50% = $5,750
Your notes say "apply the current inclusion rule". The proposed increase to 2/3 was cancelled in 2025, so the rate is 50%. Use whatever rate your instructor uses.

RRSP tax saving ≈ contribution × marginal tax rate. M6 "Maya" example: at $40,000 of income, a $2,000 RRSP contribution saves about $300 of tax.

Income attribution M1, M3

Definition: attribution rules can send investment income or capital gains back to the person who transferred or loaned the property to a spouse or common-law partner, or to certain family members such as minor children. They exist to stop a high earner from simply handing money to a lower-income relative to shift investment income to a lower tax rate.

M1 "Morgan and Alex" case (Morgan earns $180,000, Alex earns $35,000, and Morgan has $80,000 to invest)
OptionResult
1. Gift to Alex for a non-registered accountAttribution applies. Income and gains are taxed to Morgan, so splitting doesn't work.
2. Gift so Alex funds Alex's own TFSAWorks. Growth inside the TFSA is tax-free, so there's nothing to attribute. Alex needs TFSA room.
3. Prescribed-rate loan to AlexWorks if the loan is documented, charges at least the prescribed rate and the interest is paid by Jan 30. Income is then taxed to Alex.

Prescribed-rate loan rules M1 Jan 30: hint sheet

  1. The loan must be properly documented in a written loan agreement.
  2. It must charge interest of at least the prescribed rate in effect when the loan is made. That rate stays locked in for the life of the loan.
  3. Interest for each year must be paid no later than January 30 of the following year. If a payment is missed, attribution applies for that year and every later year.
  4. Tax reporting:
    • The lender reports the interest received as income.
    • The borrower reports the investment income.
    • The borrower can generally deduct the interest paid, because the money was borrowed to earn investment income.
Illustration (assume a 3% prescribed rate; check the current rate)
Morgan lends Alex $80,000 at 3% → Alex pays Morgan $2,400 by Jan 30 Alex invests and earns $4,800 Alex: reports $4,800 − deducts $2,400 interest = $2,400 net (taxed at Alex's LOW rate) Morgan: reports $2,400 interest income (taxed at Morgan's high rate) Net effect: $2,400 of investment income moves from Morgan's rate to Alex's

6 · Written-response preparation

Use this 5-step structure for every answer
  1. Define the concept.
  2. State the rule.
  3. Apply it to the facts.
  4. Give an example or calculation.
  5. Explain the planning importance.

Model answer A: RRSP and tuition (flagged on the hint sheet)

Question: Sam has a 30% marginal tax rate, contributes $3,000 to an RRSP and pays $6,000 of eligible tuition. Explain how each affects Sam's taxes.

  1. Define:
    • An RRSP contribution is a deduction: it reduces taxable income.
    • Tuition generates a non-refundable tax credit: it reduces tax payable. Tuition is not an income deduction.
  2. Rule:
    • A deduction's value depends on the marginal rate: saving ≈ contribution × marginal rate.
    • The federal tuition credit = eligible tuition × 15%.
    • A non-refundable credit can't reduce tax below zero. Unused tuition carries forward indefinitely, or up to $5,000 can be transferred.
  3. Apply:
    • Sam deducts the $3,000 RRSP contribution, which lowers taxable income.
    • Sam then claims the tuition credit against the federal tax calculated.
  4. Calculation:
    RRSP: $3,000 × 30% ≈ $900 tax saved (taxable income falls by $3,000) Tuition: $6,000 × 15% = $900 federal credit (federal tax payable falls by $900)
  5. Planning importance:
    • RRSP deductions are worth more to higher earners, so it can pay to contribute now and deduct in a higher-income year.
    • Tuition credits are worth the same 15% to everyone. A student with little tax can carry the credit forward or transfer it.
    • Remember: a deduction reduces taxable income; a credit reduces tax payable.

Model answer B: income attribution and prescribed-rate loans

  1. Define: attribution rules return investment income or gains on property that was transferred or loaned to a spouse or certain family members back to the transferor.
  2. Rule:
    • A gift to a spouse for non-registered investing → the income is attributed back to the giver.
    • Exception: a documented loan at a rate of at least the prescribed rate set at inception, with the interest paid by January 30 of the following year.
  3. Apply: use the Morgan ($180,000) and Alex ($35,000) facts from section 5.
  4. Calculation: use the $80,000-at-3% illustration in section 5.
  5. Planning importance: this legally shifts investment income to the lower-rate spouse. Missing the Jan 30 deadline even once brings attribution back permanently. Funding the spouse's TFSA is a simpler alternative.

Model answer C: avoidance, evasion and GAAR

  1. Define:
    • Avoidance: legal planning to reduce tax.
    • Evasion: deliberately breaking the law to avoid tax that is legally owed.
  2. Rule: GAAR lets the CRA deny a tax benefit when a transaction technically follows the legislation but abuses its object, spirit or purpose.
  3. Apply and example:
    • An RRSP contribution is legitimate planning.
    • A contrived series of transactions with no commercial purpose risks GAAR.
    • Not reporting cash tips is evasion.
  4. Planning importance:
    • Advisors should recommend only planning with genuine purpose and proper documentation.
    • They should never assist with concealment.
    • Evasion brings penalties and possible prosecution.

8 · Last-minute cram list

  • Marginal = next dollar · Average = tax ÷ income · EMTR = tax + lost benefits
  • Municipalities → property tax. The main purpose of tax is to fund government programs.
  • Avoidance is legal (but GAAR can deny it if it abuses the law's purpose) · Evasion is illegal
  • Filing deadline April 30; self-employed and their spouses June 15, but balances owing are due April 30
  • Filing with $0 tax still gets you refunds, benefits, credits, RRSP room and loss balances
  • Normal reassessment period ≈ 3 years · to fix an omitted amount → adjustment request
  • T4 employment · T5 interest and dividends · T3 trusts and mutual funds · T2200 employer certifies · T777 employee claims · T1-M moving · Sched 3 capital gains · Sched 7 RRSP
  • Non-taxable: inheritance, lottery, life-insurance death benefit, TFSA withdrawal. The earnings on them are taxable.
  • Deduction → taxable income (× marginal rate) · Credit → tax payable (× 15%) · Non-refundable credits can't go below $0
  • Child support: neither deductible nor taxable · Spousal support needs a written agreement or court order, periodic payments and living apart
  • Home office: principal place of work (>50%) OR exclusive use with regular and continuous client meetings
  • Interest is 100% included · Capital losses offset capital gains only, not employment income
  • RRSP: deductible in, taxable out · TFSA: not deductible in, tax-free out
  • Prescribed-rate loan: written, at least the prescribed rate at inception, interest paid by Jan 30. The lender reports the interest; the borrower reports the income and deducts the interest.
