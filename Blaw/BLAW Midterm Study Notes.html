Notes

Definition & categories of law

  • Law = the body of rules that can be enforced by the courts or government agencies. (Exam tip: law is defined by what courts will enforce, not by what people "ought" to do.)
  • Substantive law – the actual rights and rules that govern behaviour and set limits on conduct.
  • Procedural law – the rules for how substantive laws get enforced (e.g. how to sue).
  • Public law – your relationship with the government: constitutional, criminal, administrative law.
  • Private law – relationships between individuals/businesses: torts, contracts.
  • Law ≠ morality. A legal wrong isn't always a moral wrong and vice versa — but they often overlap (theft is both).

Two legal systems in Canada

  • Common-law system – English Canada, England, former British colonies. Based on court cases; judges follow precedent.
  • Civil-law system – Quebec, France, Germany, etc. Based on a written Civil Code. Judges are not bound by prior cases (may be influenced). Quebec's Civil Code covers private disputes; criminal law in Quebec is still federal.
  • ⚠ "Civil law" has two meanings: (1) the Quebec/French system, and (2) private law about money compensation, as opposed to criminal law.

Criminal vs. civil wrongs

Criminal wrongCivil wrong
AgainstThe stateAnother individual
PartiesCrown Prosecutor v. Accused (R. v. Smith)Plaintiff v. Defendant
PurposePunish, deter, rehabilitateCompensate for harm ($)
ProofBeyond a reasonable doubtBalance of probabilities
  • A civil wrong creates a cause of action — facts that entitle the plaintiff to sue. Remedies: damages ($), injunction, etc.
  • Four types of civil wrongs: tort (negligence, defamation, assault, trespass), breach of contract, statutory wrong (e.g. breaching the Residential Tenancy Act), equitable wrong (the "catch-all", e.g. fraud, breach of trust).
  • One act can be both a crime and a tort (e.g. assault, impaired driving that wrecks a house).

The common law & stare decisis

  • Origins: King Henry II's travelling courts followed local custom and borrowed from Roman law, Church law and the Law Merchant. No codes, so judges followed earlier decisions.
  • Stare decisis = "stand by what has been decided" — following precedent. A prior decision is binding when:
    1. the facts are similar enough,
    2. it's from the same jurisdiction (province), and
    3. it's from a higher court. (BC Court of Appeal binds BC Supreme Court; Supreme Court of Canada binds everyone.)
    A Nova Scotia or Alberta court decision is only persuasive in BC, never binding. A lower court never binds a higher one.
  • Lawyers argue by similarity ("same facts → same result") or distinguish the facts ("my facts are different"). Example: Mom says Jack couldn't play out after dark at 11 (similarity); Tom says he's bigger and has a green belt (distinguishing).
  • Pro: predictable & stable → people know when to settle and can plan business decisions. Con: can be harsh and inflexible.
  • Equity – the Court of Chancery decided cases on fairness to escape the common law's rigidity. In the 19th century the courts merged, so today the same courts give both common-law damages and equitable remedies (e.g. injunctions, specific performance).
  • Statutes override judge-made law; a new contrary statute beats a court precedent.

Summary

Law is the set of rules courts and government will enforce. English Canada uses the common-law system, where judges follow precedent (stare decisis) from higher courts in the same province, while Quebec uses a Civil Code. Civil wrongs (torts, contract, statutory, equitable) are about compensating victims; criminal wrongs are about punishment.

Notes

What a constitution is

  • Constitutional law – the law for interpreting and applying the Constitution. All federal and provincial laws must conform to it; courts can declare an inconsistent law of no force or effect ("strike it down" / "unconstitutional").
  • A constitution trumps other laws, entrenches rights, and is very hard to change.
  • Hierarchy of law: Constitution (supreme law) → Statute law (Acts of Parliament & Legislatures) → Judge-made law (common law & equity). Statutes override cases on the same issue; the Constitution overrides both.

Conventions inherited from Britain

  • Democratic parliamentary system; Supremacy of Parliament; the Rule of Law; principles from the Magna Carta (1215) and English Bill of Rights.
  • Rule of Law: everyone — rulers included — is subject to the same known, published laws. Government can't act arbitrarily (no arbitrary arrest), courts can review government action, people elect their government, and there's a supreme constitution. (Opposite of "divine right of kings".) Embodied in Charter s.7 (life, liberty, security of the person) and s.9 (no arbitrary detention).
  • Parliamentary supremacy is no longer complete in Canada — the Charter limits it.

Constitution Act, 1867 (formerly the BNA Act)

  • Created the Dominion of Canada; a federal system with two levels of government.
  • Divides government into three branches:
    BranchWhoMakes
    LegislativeParliament / provincial Legislative AssembliesStatute law (legislation)
    ExecutivePM/Premier, cabinet, civil servantsSubordinate legislation – regulations, municipal bylaws; implements law
    JudicialJudgesCase law; interprets law
  • Division of powers (Table 2.1):
    Federal – s.91Provincial – s.92
    Trade & commerceMunicipal institutions
    Employment insuranceHospitals / health care
    Any mode of taxationDirect taxation within the province
    Criminal law (not its enforcement)Administration of justice in the province
    Banking, currency, postal service, military, railwaysProperty and civil rights; education
    Indians and lands reserved for IndiansManagement/sale of public lands
    Residual power – POGGMatters of a local or private nature
  • POGG ("Peace, Order and Good Government") – the federal government's residual power over anything not assigned.
  • Paramountcy – if federal and provincial laws merely overlap incidentally, both stand. If they truly conflict, the federal law prevails.
  • A law passed by the wrong level of government is ultra vires (beyond its power) and invalid — e.g. a town council can't create a "crime" because criminal law is federal.

Constitution Act, 1982

  • Lists the statutes with constitutional status; ended ties with the British Parliament (no more British amendment needed).
  • Contains the Charter of Rights and Freedoms.
  • Amending formula — deliberately hard: Senate + House of Commons + legislatures of at least two-thirds of the provinces (7) representing at least 50% of the population. (So Parliament alone cannot amend the Charter.)
  • s.35 recognizes and affirms existing Indigenous and treaty rights. Self-government is seen as an inherent, pre-existing right; the Royal Proclamation of 1763 set a nation-to-nation relationship; see also UNDRIP. Businesses must respect Indigenous rights and title — failing to consult can be costly.

Summary

The Constitution is Canada's supreme law: it beats statutes, which beat judge-made law. The Constitution Act, 1867 splits law-making power between Ottawa (s.91, plus POGG) and the provinces (s.92), with federal law winning true conflicts (paramountcy). The Constitution Act, 1982 added the Charter and a hard-to-use amending formula.

Charter of Rights and Freedoms

Concept outline

  • ▶The Charter
    • ▶Applies to government only

    • s.11 rights when charged

Notes

What the Charter does

  • Entrenches individual rights (protected by the hard amending formula).
  • Protects people from rights infringements by governments and their agents/agencies — not by private individuals or businesses.
  • Lets people argue in court that a law is contrary to the Charter → trumps parliamentary supremacy and gave judges more power.

The rights

  • Fundamental freedoms (s.2) – apply to everyone in Canada (citizen or not, individual or corporation): (a) conscience and religion; (b) thought, belief, opinion and expression, incl. the press; (c) peaceful assembly; (d) association.
  • Democratic rights (s.3–5) · Mobility rights (s.6) · Legal rights (s.7–14, e.g. life/liberty/security, no unreasonable search & seizure, no arbitrary detention) · Equality rights (s.15, no discrimination) · Language rights (s.16–23).

Limits on Charter rights

  • Section 1 (reasonable limits) – government may limit a right if it's justified in a free and democratic society.
  • Section 33 (notwithstanding clause) – a legislature can pass a law that operates "notwithstanding" the Charter, but must say so expressly and review/re-pass it every 5 years. It cannot override democratic or mobility rights (it only covers s.2 and s.7–15).
  • The Charter applies only to governments, their agents, and legislation.

Enforcement

  • Section 24 – anyone whose rights are violated can go to court for a remedy.
  • Section 52 – the Constitution is the supreme law; any inconsistent law is, to the extent of the inconsistency, of no force or effect.

Section 11 – rights when charged with a criminal offence

  • Be informed of the charge · trial within a reasonable time · not be a witness against yourself · presumed innocent until proven guilty · reasonable bail · jury trial if penalty is 5+ years · no double jeopardy (can't be tried twice for the same crime).

Human rights legislation

  • Protects against discrimination in private relationships (e.g. employer–employee, landlord–tenant) on grounds like gender, religion, ethnic origin, race, age, disability.
  • Federal: Canadian Human Rights Act. BC: BC Human Rights Code. Complaints go to tribunals (e.g. Human Rights Tribunal); courts can do judicial review if natural justice isn't followed. Must comply with the Charter.
  • The older Canadian Bill of Rights tried to protect people from government but ultimately couldn't (just an ordinary statute).
  • Rule of thumb: hurt by a person/business → human rights complaint. Hurt by a law or government action → Charter challenge in court.

Summary

The Charter protects individuals from government (not from private parties) by guaranteeing freedoms and rights that courts can enforce under s.24 and s.52. Rights can be limited if justified in a free and democratic society (s.1) or overridden for 5 renewable years with the notwithstanding clause (s.33). Discrimination by private parties is handled by human rights legislation and tribunals.

Concept outline

Notes

Two ways to resolve a civil wrong

  • Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) – resolving disputes outside the courts.
  • Litigation – "go to court".

The three ADR options

NegotiationMediationArbitration
Who decides?The parties themselves ("self-help")The parties — mediator only helpsThe arbitrator — solution is imposed
Binding?Agreement may not be legally bindingOnly if parties agree; court may affirm itYes — binding on the parties
NotesCooperation & compromise; good communication; may use representatives; can improve the relationshipNeutral outsider finds common ground, encourages concessions; sometimes required before courtOften required by contract; arbitrators are often specialists; cannot be appealed to courts (provincial arbitration statutes), but courts can review procedure ("due process")

Advantages of ADR Textbook + slides

  • Private/confidential – no public record, protects reputation and trade secrets.
  • Faster and cheaper than litigation.
  • Flexible – parties control timing, process and creative solutions (especially negotiation/mediation).
  • Less adversarial → better for maintaining goodwill and ongoing business relationships (a good reason to mediate).
  • Arbitrators can be experts in the subject matter.

Disadvantages of ADR

  • Negotiated/mediated outcomes may not be binding or easily enforceable; a failed process adds cost and delay.
  • No precedent is set; no public vindication.
  • Arbitration: very limited right to appeal; more formal and adversarial than mediation.
  • Works poorly where parties don't trust each other or there's a big power imbalance.

Litigation, by comparison

  • Advantages: binding judgment, direct enforcement mechanisms, right to appeal, procedural protections, sets precedent.
  • Disadvantages: expensive, slow, public, adversarial (damages relationships), complex — you lose control to the judge.

Summary

ADR resolves disputes outside court through negotiation (parties decide), mediation (a neutral helper, no decision) or arbitration (a binding decision by a third party). ADR is generally private, faster, cheaper, more flexible and better at preserving relationships. Litigation is slower, costlier and public, but produces enforceable, appealable judgments.

Notes

Court hierarchy (BC)

Supreme Court of Canada (final court)
Federal Court of AppealCourt of Appeal of BC
Federal Court (trial)Supreme Court of BC
Provincial Court

Trial courts of the provinces

  • Provincial Court – lower trial court. Includes Small Claims Court for claims up to $35,000; also most criminal, family and youth matters. Simpler procedure than the Supreme Court. Textbook
  • Supreme Court of BC – the superior trial court; unlimited monetary jurisdiction (e.g. a $500,000 negligence claim goes here). Despite the name, it's a trial court, not the top court.

Provincial Courts of Appeal Textbook

  • Court of Appeal of BC hears appeals from the trial courts, usually by a panel of (typically 3) judges.
  • No new trial: no witnesses or new evidence — it reviews whether the trial judge made errors of law (rarely overturns findings of fact).
  • Its decisions bind all BC trial courts. It is not bound by the BC Supreme Court (lower court). It is bound by the Supreme Court of Canada.
  • The Supreme Court of Canada hears further appeals, mostly only with leave (permission), on issues of national importance.

Criminal vs. civil function

  • Criminal (R. v. Accused): the Crown must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt (BARD). Victims are only witnesses and aren't usually compensated.
  • Civil (Plaintiff v. Defendant): decided on a balance of probabilities (BOP) — more likely than not. An acquittal in criminal court does not prevent a civil lawsuit.

Lawsuit procedure

  • Pre-trial checks: limitation periods (you may be too late!); jurisdiction — where the defendant lives or harm occurred ("real and substantial connection"); which court (based on $ at stake); try negotiation first.
  • With a lawyer: demand letter → pleadings filed in the court registry:
    • Notice of Civil Claim (plaintiff) – facts, relief sought, legal basis.
    • Response to Civil Claim (defendant) – due within 21 days if served in Canada (35 USA, 49 elsewhere). Miss it → default judgment.
    • Counterclaim – the defendant sues the plaintiff back.
  • Chambers applications; discovery of documents and examination for discovery (parties questioned under oath — admissions can be used at trial); payment into court.
  • Trial: plaintiff then defendant present their cases with direct examination and cross-examination of witnesses; rules of evidence apply (e.g. general rule against hearsay).

Judgment

  • Judge makes findings of fact, applies the law (cases + statutes), gives reasons and a judgment.
  • Remedies if the plaintiff wins:
    • General damages – pain & suffering, wage loss.
    • Special damages – reimburse actual expenses.
    • Punitive damages – punish high-handed conduct.
    • An accounting (of profits), an injunction, specific performance.
    • Costs – a contribution to the winner's legal fees.

Enforcement (after trial)

  • Winning doesn't mean getting paid — the judgment must be enforced:
  • Examination in aid of execution – questioning the debtor to find assets.
  • Seizure of property · registering the judgment against land · Garnishment (taking money from wages/bank accounts) · injunction (order to stop).

Summary

In BC, small claims (up to $35,000) start in Provincial Court, larger claims go to the Supreme Court of BC, and appeals go to the BC Court of Appeal and then the Supreme Court of Canada. A civil case moves from pleadings and discovery to trial, where the plaintiff must prove the case on a balance of probabilities. A winning plaintiff can get damages, costs or equitable remedies, then enforce them through seizure or garnishment.

Procedural Fairness in Tribunals

Concept outline

  • ▶Administrative law
    • ▶Tribunals created by statute
      • e.g. Workers' Compensation Board
    • Rule of Law – officials need legal authority
    • ▶Rules of natural justice
      • Fair hearing: notice, see evidence, be heard
      • Heard by the decision maker
      • Impartiality – no bias
    • ▶Judicial review
      • Ultra vires? Charter? Natural justice?
      • Remedies: certiorari, prohibition, mandamus

Cues

  1. What is an administrative tribunal?
  2. Why use tribunals instead of courts?
  3. Three rules of natural justice?
  4. Is there a right to a lawyer?
  5. What is judicial review?
  6. What is mandamus?

Notes

Administrative law

  • Administrative law (public law) covers people's rights when dealing with government regulatory bodies.
  • An administrative tribunal is created by statute along with a regulatory system — e.g. the Workers Compensation Act creates the WCB. WCB is no-fault: an injured worker can't sue the employer and gets WCB compensation instead.
  • Officials must have legal authority for what they do (Rule of Law) — their position alone doesn't let them infringe rights.
  • Tribunals: specialized expertise, more discretion than judges, quicker and cheaper. Court rules of evidence and procedure need not be followed.

The rules of natural justice (procedural fairness)

  1. Fair hearing – reasonable notice of the hearing; opportunity to see and respond to all evidence; opportunity to be heard (produce evidence and witnesses; cross-examine if that's the only way to defend yourself).
  2. Heard by the decision maker – those deciding must be the ones who heard the evidence.
  3. Impartiality – no bias by decision makers.

⚠ The right to have a lawyer is not automatically a rule of natural justice.

Judicial review

  • A complainant denied a fair hearing can apply for judicial review by the courts. Textbook Courts ask: Is the enabling statute valid (not ultra vires; Charter-compliant)? Did the tribunal act within its statutory power? Did it follow natural justice? Courts do not ask whether they'd have made the same decision.
  • Remedies Textbook: certiorari (quash the decision), prohibition (stop a tribunal acting beyond its power), mandamus (force a decision maker to make a decision she's been avoiding).

Summary

Administrative tribunals like the WCB are created by statute to make quick, expert decisions, but they must follow the rules of natural justice: a fair hearing, decided by those who heard the evidence, without bias. If they don't, the affected person can ask a court for judicial review.

Torts, Vicarious Liability & Trespass

Notes

The nature of torts

  • A tort is a civil wrong that causes harm to another. It can be intentional (deliberate) or unintentional (careless/accidental = negligence).
  • A crime is a wrong against society; a tort is a wrong against an individual. Some acts are both.
  • Purpose: to compensate victims ($), putting them in the position they'd have been in had the tort not occurred. Courts may add punitive damages in intentional tort cases.
  • Max award for pain and suffering in Canada ≈ $470,000 (2026). Trespass to land, defamation and battery are actionable even with no loss ("actionable without proof of damage").

Vicarious liability

  • Vicarious liability = liability without personal fault (a form of strict liability). An employer is liable for torts its employees commit in the course of employment.
  • Both the employee and the employer can be sued — e.g. a travel agent's careless advice exposes both him and his agency. Customer altercations can expose the business.

Trespass to person: assault & battery

  • Assault – causing fear of imminent contact (includes threats); protects peace of mind. Throwing a cup that misses = assault.
  • Battery – deliberate physical contact without consent; contact need not be harmful. Includes the right to refuse medical treatment — requires informed consent.
  • Defences: (1) accidental contact (no intent); (2) consent (informed); (3) self-defence with reasonable force.

False imprisonment

  • False imprisonment – unlawful, intentional restraint of a person against their will, without lawful justification. Restraint can be physical or psychological. Justified only if the person did something they can be arrested for (e.g. shoplifting → citizen's arrest, Criminal Code s.494).

Trespass to land

  • Trespass to land – going onto someone's land without permission or lawful right (or leaving things there).
  • No harm needed ("bruising one's grass" is enough). No intent to trespass needed — ignorance of the property line is no excuse.
  • Permission can be implied (businesses invite customers in; postal carriers, meter readers).
  • Occupier owes only a minimal duty to a trespasser, unless the trespasser is a minor. Lack of control can be a defence for the parents.

Trespass to chattels, conversion & detinue

TortWhat it isExample
Trespass to chattelsDeliberate damage to someone's goodsKicking the door of my car
ConversionWrongfully taking/appropriating goods (incl. theft)Borrowing my mower and selling it
DetinueWrongfully keeping goodsBorrowing my mower with permission, then refusing to return it

Nuisance

  • Private nuisance – using your property in a way that interferes with a neighbour's use and enjoyment of theirs (e.g. the neighbour's bees swarming the pool).
  • Public nuisance affects the public generally; usually the government sues.

Summary

A tort is a civil wrong against an individual, intentional or careless, and tort law aims to compensate the victim. Employers are vicariously liable for employees' torts committed on the job. Intentional torts include assault (fear of contact), battery (unconsented contact), false imprisonment, trespass to land (no harm needed), and wrongs to goods: trespass to chattels (damage), conversion (taking) and detinue (keeping).

  5. What is injurious falsehood (product defamation)?
  6. What is passing off?

Notes

Defamation

  • Defamation – a published false statement about someone that damages their reputation.
  • Must be published — communicated to a third party. Insulting a friend in a private text to him isn't defamation.
  • Slander = spoken; Libel = written (special concern online — lies on Facebook = libel).
  • Defences:
    1. Truth (justification) – works every time.
    2. Absolute privilege – statements in court or the legislature (politicians' debates).
    3. Qualified privilege – statements made as part of job duties (e.g. a reference letter), in good faith.
    4. Fair comment – honest opinion/criticism on matters of public interest (e.g. a letter criticizing the mayor).
    5. Responsible communication on matters of public importance (journalism).

Product defamation (injurious falsehood)

  • Injurious falsehood (product defamation) – telling lies maliciously and without just cause about a product or business that cause it harm (the "chocolate milk tort").
  • Defamation protects a person's reputation; injurious falsehood protects a product's or business's.

Other business torts

  • Inducing breach of contract – e.g. head-hunting an employee to break their contract.
  • Interference with economic relations – e.g. kickbacks or bribes to staff to switch suppliers.
  • Conspiracy – two or more people acting together to do the above. Intimidation – threats of violence or illegal strikes.
  • Intentional infliction of mental suffering – e.g. harassment around a wrongful dismissal.
  • Deceit (civil fraud) – intentionally misleading someone, causing damage.
  • Passing off – misrepresenting your goods as someone else's (improper use of a trademark/get-up) at common law.
  • Breach of confidence – misusing confidential information given in confidence (lawyer gives client's secret recipe to his wife).

Privacy

  • Intrusion upon seclusion – a privacy tort, not yet firmly established. Privacy is mainly protected by statute.
  • PIPEDA (federal) regulates collecting and using personal information; BC's Personal Information Protection Act; BC Privacy Act s.3 – can't use someone's portrait for commercial purposes without consent.
  • Employee surveillance may violate privacy and human rights laws.

Summary

Defamation is a false statement, communicated to a third party, that harms someone's reputation: slander if spoken, libel if written. The defences are truth, absolute privilege, qualified privilege, fair comment and responsible communication. Product defamation (injurious falsehood) is a malicious lie about a product or business; other business torts include inducing breach of contract, passing off and deceit.

What is negligence?

  • Negligence – inadvertent, careless conduct that causes injury to another. Intent doesn't matter, so "I didn't mean to" is not a defence. A major area of liability for professionals.

The four elements (Table 5.1) — the plaintiff must prove ALL four

ElementTest
ADuty of care owed to the plaintiff1. Reasonable foreseeability test (foreseeable plaintiff) — we owe a duty to anyone we can reasonably foresee might be harmed. 2. Policy considerations may negate it (Anns test).
BBreach of the standard of careReasonable person test – what would a reasonably prudent person with all the facts have done? Reasonable care, not perfection. Professionals are held to the standard of a reasonable member of their profession.
CCausationPhysical: the “but for” test ("but for the defendant's carelessness, the injury wouldn't have happened"). Legal: the remoteness test — was the injury reasonably foreseeable?
DDamageA real loss of a type courts recognize: physical harm, a mental disorder (not mere distress), economic loss.

The Anns test (fine-tuning duty)

  1. Is it reasonably foreseeable that the careless act would injure this plaintiff? If yes, a duty exists…
  2. …unless there's a social/policy reason the defendant shouldn't be liable. (E.g. social hosts — Childs v. Desormeaux.)

Thin skull rule

  • Thin skull rule – "take your victim as you find them." You're liable for the full extent of the harm, even if the victim was unusually vulnerable (e.g. a 70-year-old cyclist with a rare bone disease).

Defences to negligence

  • Contributory negligence – the plaintiff was partly careless. Damages are reduced by the plaintiff's share (40% at fault → recovers only 60%). It does not wipe out the defendant's liability.
  • Volenti non fit injuria (voluntary assumption of risk) – the plaintiff voluntarily took on an obvious risk, both physical and legal. If it succeeds, the plaintiff gets $0. Hard to prove (a passenger who took the physical but not the legal risk still recovers).

Remedies

  • Courts try to restore the victim to the position they'd have been in without the negligence.

Misfeasance, nonfeasance & rescuers

  • Misfeasance – an act that causes harm → courts give a remedy.
  • Nonfeasance – failing to act to prevent injury → courts are reluctant, except with special relationships (parent–child, doctor–patient, employer–employee).
  • If you do help, you must not be grossly negligent. BC's Good Samaritan Act: emergency helpers aren't liable unless grossly negligent (doesn't protect people paid to help or helping for gain).
  • Children are liable for their own torts (judged against a reasonable child of that age); parents are liable only for failure to control/supervise, or by statute.

Summary

To win in negligence, a plaintiff must prove a duty of care (reasonable foreseeability, subject to Anns policy limits), a breach of the reasonable-person standard, causation (but-for and not too remote) and an actual loss. Under the thin skull rule, defendants pay for the full harm. Contributory negligence reduces damages, while volenti (voluntary assumption of the legal risk) eliminates them.

Notes

Occupier's liability

  • An occupier (owner or tenant in possession) owes visitors a duty to take reasonable care to keep them reasonably safe (BC Occupiers Liability Act, a provincial statute).
  • Trespassers committing crimes are deemed to assume the risk, but the occupier still can't wilfully create a danger or show reckless disregard for their safety.
  • Child trespassers get more protection — think "allurements" like an old fridge in the yard that a child climbs into.

Commercial and social host liability

  • Commercial hosts (bars, restaurants) have a positive duty to keep intoxicated guests from being injured or injuring others.
  • Social hosts at home generally do not — the duty is limited for policy reasons (Anns test; Childs v. Desormeaux). An office-party host's duty may not extend to every stranger on the road.

Strict liability

  • Strict liability = liability without fault — no carelessness or intent needed.
  • When a dangerous situation is created by an unusual use of property, the owner/occupier is liable for all damage if the danger escapes (e.g. stored water, dangerous animals/dogs, dangerous substances). Vicarious liability is also a form of strict liability.

Product liability

  • Donoghue v. Stevenson (UK, the snail in the ginger beer) set key precedents:
    • the foreseeability test for duty of care (the "neighbour" principle);
    • privity of contract doesn't defeat a negligence claim — you can sue a manufacturer even if you didn't buy the product;
    • manufacturers owe a duty of care to the ultimate consumer.
  • Product liability in Canada: the plaintiff must prove the manufacturer was negligent (not automatic/strict), but breach can be inferred from circumstantial evidence.
  • Manufacturers have a duty to warn of dangers associated with the product.
  • Exam pattern: a friend who didn't buy the product (worms in the muffin, battery in the beer) can't sue the seller in contract, but can sue the manufacturer in tort.

Summary

Occupiers must keep visitors reasonably safe and must not wilfully or recklessly endanger even trespassers. Strict liability makes owners liable without fault when something dangerous escapes from an unusual use of their property. Since Donoghue v. Stevenson, anyone injured by a defective product can sue the manufacturer in negligence without a contract, and manufacturers have a duty to warn.

ℹ These two topics are on your midterm list but not in the slides. These notes come from the textbook (Business Law in Canada, Ch. 5) and the instructor's practice questions. Double-check them against your textbook.

Liability of professionals and other experts Textbook

  • Professionals (accountants, lawyers, doctors, engineers, financial advisors) can be liable to clients in contract, in tort (negligence), and for breach of fiduciary duty (a duty of utmost good faith and loyalty: put the client's interests first, avoid conflicts of interest, keep information confidential).
  • Standard of care = a reasonable professional in that field ("what would the reasonable accountant, knowing all the facts, do?"). Reasonable care, not a guarantee of results.
  • Negligent misstatement (Hedley Byrne v. Heller) – careless advice or information that someone relies on can create liability for pure economic loss.
  • Third parties (non-clients): a professional owes a duty when they know the person (or class of persons) will rely on the work for that specific purpose, and the reliance is reasonable. E.g. an auditor who knew a specific investor would rely on financial statements → liable for the investor's loss.
  • Hercules Managements v. Ernst & Young (SCC): auditors weren't liable to shareholders who used audited statements for personal investment decisions — statements were prepared for a different purpose; policy limits on indeterminate liability (Anns step 2).
  • No negligence if the work is accurate: an accountant who accurately prepared statements isn't liable even if the investor loses money. Same for a zoning officer's careless advice — a public official can be liable for negligent misstatement.
  • Professionals manage risk with errors and omissions (E&O) / liability insurance — relevant to your future as a financial planner!

Insurance and subrogation Textbook

  • Insurable interest – the insured must have a personal stake in whatever is insured (would suffer a loss if it were damaged). Otherwise the policy is void (it would just be gambling).
  • Subrogation – once an insurer pays out on a loss, it steps into the shoes of the insured and can sue the person who caused the loss (in the insured's name), to recover what it paid.
  • Example: a careless driver crashes into your garage. Your insurer pays for repairs, then sues the driver in negligence to get its money back.
  • The insured can't recover twice (from both the insurer and the wrongdoer) and must not do anything that harms the insurer's subrogation rights (e.g. releasing the wrongdoer).

Summary

Professionals owe clients duties in contract, tort and as fiduciaries, measured against a reasonable member of their profession. They can also be liable to third parties for negligent misstatement when they know the person will rely on their work for a specific purpose. Through subrogation, an insurer that has paid a claim can step into the insured's shoes and sue the wrongdoer.
