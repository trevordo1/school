How a business succeeds

Sell a good or service customers value (the value proposition) at a price that covers all costs, including the cost of capital. That requires skilled ethical people, good customer/supplier relationships, and enough cash. The firm must also earn returns high enough to attract investors.

The 3 jobs of corporate finance

  • Getting financing → capital structure decisions (debt vs equity mix).
  • Choosing investments → capital budgeting decisions. A project must cover all costs including the cost of capital, and must add value.
  • Managing day-to-day financing → working capital decisions (this is Ch 17).

Savers vs users of capital

Financing providers (savers) have more cash than they need now: retirement savers, pension funds, banks. Financing seekers (users) have opportunities but not enough cash: students, governments, entrepreneurs, growing corporations.

Savers give cash in exchange for a claim on future cash flow: interest (loans, bonds) or dividends and capital gains (common/preferred shares). This happens through financial markets. If capital is not allocated efficiently, it is hard to create wealth and economic growth.

How a saver decides: (1) forecast the project's cash flows and its expected rate of return; (2) assess risk and set the minimum acceptable return for that risk, the required rate of return; (3) invest only if expected ≥ required.

The main objective: value maximization

Value maximization = maximizing the value of the firm = maximizing shareholders' wealth = maximizing the long-run stock price.

Value of firm = PV of expected Free Cash Flows (FCF), discounted at WACC FCF = Sales − Operating costs − Operating taxes − Required investments in operating capital

Free Cash Flow (FCF) is the cash available for distribution ("free") to all investors. The Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) is the average return required by the firm's investors, combining the after-tax return lenders require and the return shareholders require.

So three things drive value: the amount, the timing and the riskiness of FCF. All else equal, investors prefer more cash, sooner, with less risk.

Is shareholder value maximization good for society?

The "yes" arguments:

  • Share ownership is widespread, so higher share value makes many people wealthier.
  • Competition among value-seeking firms makes products cheaper and better for consumers.
  • Growing firms hire more workers, which reduces unemployment.
  • Successful firms pay taxes.

The debate is not settled; there are counter-arguments to each point. Management decisions affect all stakeholders: shareholders, lenders, customers, suppliers, employees, government, community and the environment.

Governance, the law and ethics

  • US corporate law: shareholder wealth maximization takes priority.
  • Canadian law: decisions must be in the "best interests of the corporation", which includes shareholders but also considers other stakeholders.

Ethics is more than following rules: no insider trading (using confidential info for personal gain), product safety and quality, fair employment, fair marketing and selling, community involvement.

For a public company the right target is the intrinsic share price (value based on true expected cash flows), not the current market price. Misleading investors to push the price above intrinsic value temporarily is unethical.

Exam trap: "Maximize profit" or "maximize the current stock price" are wrong answers. The goal is to maximize long-run / intrinsic value.

The agency problem

Shareholders usually hire managers to run the firm (separation of ownership and management). Example: Rogers Communications has ~429 million B shares; 20–24% held by retail investors, the rest by 500+ institutions.

Managers have personal goals (power, wealth, perks). When those conflict with shareholder wealth (a new corporate jet, a fancy but unneeded HQ), you get the agency problem.

  • Direct agency costs: the cost of monitoring executives.
  • Indirect agency costs: lost opportunities and the consequences of poor decisions (e.g. bad mergers and acquisitions, M&A).

Solutions:

  • Compensation that aligns executives with shareholders: bonuses based on FCF generation, NOT sales.
  • Governance: an independent board of directors that monitors executives, sets strategy, tracks performance and protects shareholders.

Separating investment and consumption decisions

Investors who want cash nowInvestors who want growth
High dividends to fund present consumptionReinvest now for higher dividends later (delayed consumption)
Higher payout ratio, lower retained earningsLower payout ratio, higher retained earnings

Irving Fisher's Separation Theorem: the firm should always pursue the strategy that maximizes value, regardless of its investors' consumption preferences. Investors can use the capital markets (buy/sell shares, borrow, save) to match their own timing.

Summary

A firm's goal is to maximize its long-run intrinsic value, which is the present value of its future free cash flows discounted at the WACC. Value goes up with more cash, sooner and with less risk. Because managers and owners are different people, firms use FCF-based pay and independent boards to keep managers focused on value, and they invest to maximize value no matter how individual investors want their cash timed.

The capital allocation process

Capital allocation moves capital from savers (earn more than they spend) to investors (spend more than they earn to invest), on terms both accept. Borrowing to fund consumption (a vacation, a boat) is not investing.

Three routes:

  1. Direct transfer: the firm sells securities straight to the saver.
  2. Indirect transfer through investment bankers: a "middle-person" helps the firm issue and sell new securities.
  3. Indirect transfer through a financial intermediary: e.g. a commercial bank or pension fund collects savers' money and invests it.

The process involves creating new securities, financial institutions and financial markets.

Financial markets and securities

Financial markets are "where" financial assets (securities) are bought and sold. Securities are contracts that entitle the owner to part of the cash flow generated by a business's assets: stocks, bonds, loans, mortgages.

Derivatives are securities whose value comes from another asset (the underlying asset: a commodity, stock or currency). Examples: options, futures, swaps (covered in FMGT 4510).

The cost of money

The cost of money (cost of capital) is the return investors need before they'll invest. For debt it is the interest rate; for equity it is the cost of equity (dividends + capital gains).

4 fundamental factors:

  1. Production opportunities: what return can real assets and businesses earn?
  2. Time preference for consumption: how much do people prefer spending now vs saving?
  3. Risk
  4. Expected inflation

Market participants

Individuals ("retail clients") and institutions (life insurers, hedge funds, pension funds, mutual funds, governments, credit unions, commercial and investment banks). Exchange members trade directly; everyone else uses a broker, either online (Questrade, Robinhood) or a full-service investment dealer (Raymond James, BMO Nesbitt).

FinTech

FinTech = online platforms that combine financial services with technology (AI, blockchain, robo-advisors, internet of things) to give safer, faster, easier access and lower costs and fees.

  • Individuals: credit cards, mortgages, identity theft protection, credit score monitoring.
  • Businesses: loans, lines of credit, automated secure transactions.
  • Investors: portfolio management, real-time securities settlement.
  • Example: Equitable Bank's all-digital EQ Bank platform.

Recent trends

Private equity is growing. Tech value creation increasingly happens in private markets; leading AI firms raise billions without going public. Deep pools of PE/VC money delay IPOs, so private investors capture more of the growth and new public shareholders may get less upside. Example: SpaceX private round in 2023 at $80, IPO price $135.

Risk of a debt crisis. Global debt ≈ 235% of world GDP; public debt ≈ 94%. Cheap old debt must be refinanced at higher rates while governments face competing demands (defence, aging populations, infrastructure, AI, climate). Large deficits in the USA, Japan and France make it systemic. More debt → higher rates → higher financing costs, weaker growth, higher default risk. It's a debt sustainability problem rather than an imminent crash.

Summary

Capital flows from savers to investors directly, through investment bankers, or through intermediaries like banks. Its price, the cost of money, depends on production opportunities, time preference, risk and inflation, and is pushed around by central banks, government deficits, the business cycle and trade. Know the three market splits (money/capital, primary/secondary, private/public) and who trades in each.

Statements and standards

The 4 statements: Statement of Financial Position (balance sheet), Statement of Profit and Loss and OCI (income statement), Statement of Changes in Equity, Statement of Cash Flows. In Canada: IFRS. Private companies may use the simpler ASPE. A private firm might still choose IFRS if it plans to go public or its lenders/investors want comparable statements.

Balance sheet

A snapshot at one moment. Assets − Liabilities = Equity; equity is a residual claim (behind debt holders). Assets are listed by liquidity; liabilities by how soon they're due.

Weaknesses: assets at historic cost, not market value; valuable intangibles (management, reputation, talent) don't appear at all.

Income statement

Performance over a period, on an accrual basis (matching principle), so timing differs from cash. IFRS adds Other Comprehensive Income (OCI): unrealized gains/losses on investments, derivatives, FX hedges, recorded separately in equity. Non-cash items: depreciation/amortization, bad debt expense, options expense, gains/losses on asset sales.

EBIT (operating income) = profit before interest & taxes Acme 2023: 21.5 EBITDA = EBIT + Depreciation & Amortization Acme 2023: 22.7 Net Cash Flow (NCF) = NI + DA Acme 2023: 13.0

Net cash flow is a rough profit measure if the firm never replaced worn-out assets. It is not FCF.

Statement of cash flows (interpret it)

Acme 2023: operating +10.4 (operations generate cash), investing (15.1) (buying fixed assets and STI), financing +4.7 (capital raised to cover the gap). Net change 0. The operating section matters most for a healthy firm.

FCF recap

BucketItems
Operating current assetsCash, A/R, inventory
Non-operating assetsShort-term investments ("excess")
Operating current liabilitiesA/P, accruals: spontaneous, they grow automatically with sales and cost no return to investors
Investor-supplied capitalST bank loans/notes, LT debt, equity (these investors require a return)
NOWC = Operating CA − Operating CL TNOC = NOWC + Operating long-term assets NOPAT = EBIT(1 − T) FCF = NOPAT − Net investment in TNOC (TNOC this yr − TNOC last yr) ROIC = NOPAT / TNOC Acme 2023: FCF = 16.3 − 13.8 = 2.5; ROIC = 16.3/98.7 = 16.5% > 10% WACC → value created

Negative FCF is not automatically bad: a growing firm investing in assets that raise future FCF can show negative FCF today (like buying a condo instead of renting).

FCF is used to: pay after-tax interest, repay/raise debt principal, pay dividends, buy back/issue stock, and buy/sell short-term investments.

Summary

Accounting profit isn't cash: the income statement uses accruals and the balance sheet uses historic cost. FCF strips the statements down to operating items to show the cash truly available to all investors, and ROIC above WACC means the firm created value.

Where analysis starts

Financial statement analysis (FSA) evaluates and compares a firm's position and performance and helps forecast earnings, dividends and FCF (S&P Capital IQ is the pro database; BCIT library has it).

Step 1 is always the statement of cash flows: Are operations producing or using cash, and what's the trend? Is a lot of cash going to investing? Is financing bringing cash in or paying investors, and which type (debt or equity)? Step 2: ratios and FCF, and their trends.

Tools: comparative statements (this year vs past years), common-size statements, and financial ratios.

  • Inward approach: compare the firm to its own past (trend analysis).
  • Outward approach: compare to other firms in the industry. Use with caution: regional and size differences, different accounting policies and poor industry categories make true comparables hard to find.

DuPont analysis

The DuPont system links three perspectives (profitability, productivity, leverage) to explain the firm's financial strategy through ROE.

ROE = NI/Sales × Sales/TA × TA/Equity = Net profit margin × Total asset turnover × Equity multiplier = (profitability) × (productivity) × (leverage) ROA = NPM × TAT ROE = ROA × EM
  • NPM: cents of after-tax profit per $1 of sales.
  • TAT: dollars of sales per $1 of assets.
  • EM: dollars of assets per $1 of equity. Higher EM = more debt financing = more risk.

How to explain the change: ROE rose by about 3.6 points. The driver is profitability (NPM up 4.1 points, helped by COGS falling from 62.4% to 58.2% of sales). Asset use improved slightly (TAT 0.62 → 0.64). Leverage actually fell (EM 1.467 → 1.385, as LT debt was paid down), so the higher ROE came from better operations, not from taking on more debt risk. That's a "good quality" ROE increase.

Exam technique: when asked to explain ROE, name the component that moved the most, say whether it's profitability, productivity or leverage, and point out whether the ROE gain came with more risk (EM up) or not.

Quick check of 2024: NPM = 240/2,075 = 11.57%; TAT = 2,075/3,373 = 0.615; EM = 3,373/2,299 = 1.467; product ≈ 10.44%.

Common-size and percent-change analysis

  • Common-size statements (vertical analysis): balance sheet items ÷ total assets; income statement items ÷ sales.
  • Percent change analysis: % growth in each item over time.
  • Trend analysis: plot a ratio over several years (e.g. Ellington ROE 2019–2023).

Ellington examples: A/R rose from 4.9% to 5.2% of total assets; COGS fell from 62.4% to 58.2% of sales; LT debt fell from 15.7% to 12.7% of assets.

Limitations of FSA

  • Balance sheet uses historic cost, not market value, so ratios may mean little and may not reflect cash-generating ability.
  • Different accounting policies make comparisons hard.
  • Window dressing: firms make statements look better (e.g. around year-end).
  • Highly summarized statements with little detail.
  • High inflation or deflation distorts results.
  • Conglomerates span many industries, so industry comparisons are hard.
Summary

DuPont splits ROE into net profit margin, total asset turnover and the equity multiplier so you can see whether returns come from profit, efficient asset use or leverage. Ellington's ROE rose because margins improved while leverage fell. Ratios are only as good as the accounting numbers behind them, so know the main limitations.

Why money has time value

Waiting a year for $100 means taking risks (default risk; inflation cuts purchasing power) and making sacrifices (delayed consumption; can't invest it and earn interest). That's an opportunity cost, so you'd only wait if paid interest. At 4%, $100 today = $104 in a year.

Timeline rules: t = 0 is today; t = 1 is end of period 1 / start of period 2; outflows negative, inflows positive. Always draw one first.

Compound: FV_N = PV(1 + I)^N $1,000 at 10% for 3 yrs = $1,331 Simple: FV_N = PV(1 + N·I) $1,000 at 10% for 3 yrs = $1,300

The $31 difference is compound interest (interest on interest). The gap grows with time ($3,727.5 after 20 years). Simple interest is used only for short periods (≤ 1 year). Assume compound unless told otherwise.

Three rates

  • Nominal (quoted) rate / APR: what the bank quotes. Ignores compounding; only use it directly if compounding is once a year.
  • Periodic rate = INOM / M (M = compounding periods per year).
  • Effective annual rate (EAR / EFF): the true annual rate after compounding. Lets you compare APRs with different compounding.
EAR = (1 + APR/M)^M − 1 Continuous: EAR = e^APR − 1 12% compounded monthly: (1 + 0.12/12)^12 − 1 = 12.6825%

Loans

Amortized loans are repaid with fixed blended payments (interest + principal) until the balance is zero. Early payments are mostly interest; as principal is repaid, the interest portion shrinks and the principal portion grows.

Non-amortized: interest-only (bullet) loan — only interest during the term, all principal in one lump sum at maturity. Balloon loan — smaller regular payments, leaving a large final "balloon" payment.

By law, Canadian fixed-rate mortgages compound semi-annually, but payments are usually monthly, so payment and compounding frequencies differ.

Overview

Working capital management answers: (1) What is the right amount of operating working capital? (2) How should it be financed? It goes beyond finance: logistics, operations, engineering and IT all affect it, because every operating step touches a working capital account.

ActivityAccounts affected
Purchase raw materialsCash, Inventory, Payables
Produce goodsCash, Inventory, Payables, Accruals
Sell goodsCash, Receivables, Inventory
Collect receivablesCash, Receivables
Pay payablesCash, Payables

Cash = short-term financial assets held to support ongoing operations. Short-term investments = short-term financial assets held for future purposes. Consistent with the Ch 2 FCF/NOWC definitions (may differ from strict accounting). A cash equivalent is normally a debt security with < 3 months to maturity; a short-term investment has > 3 months. Both are liquid.

PolicyWhat it holdsTrade-offSuits
RelaxedLots of cash, inventory, A/R; little A/P and accrualsFewer stock-outs and lost sales, but lots of NOWC to finance → lower ROI. Low risk, low returnHard-to-predict demand, long operating cycle, need a cushion (e.g. supermarket)
RestrictedLittle current assets; lots of A/P and accrualsHigher risk of stock-outs and lost sales, but less NOWC → higher ROI. High risk, high returnPredictable demand, short operating cycle, tight inventories (utilities, airlines, service firms)
ModerateBetween the two extremes

Temporary vs permanent NOWC

Temporary NOWC rises and falls with seasons (ski or toy manufacturers). Permanent NOWC is the base level always needed (heavy equipment maker, not seasonal). Permanent NOWC grows as the business grows.

Financing policies

  • Maturity matching ("self-liquidating"), also called the moderate approach: permanent NOWC (and fixed assets) with LT debt/equity; temporary NOWC with ST debt. Match asset life to financing term. You wouldn't fund a 30-year mine with a 1-year loan, or 50 days of inventory with a 5-year loan.
  • Aggressive: ST debt funds all temporary NOWC and some or all permanent NOWC. Might save interest (ST rates are usually lower than LT rates) but high rollover risk: the ST loan may not be renewed.
  • Conservative: LT debt funds all permanent NOWC and some or all temporary NOWC. Rollover risk is reduced but interest cost is likely higher, and you need to invest the excess cash when temporary NOWC is at its low point.

The cash conversion cycle

How many days your cash is stuck in operations, how to calculate it, and how much cash you free up by shortening it.

Concept outline
  • The cycle a firm follows
    • Buy inventory → sell goods
    • Pay payables, collect receivables
  • Measure it in days
    • ICP: days inventory is held
    • DSO: days to collect from customers
    • APP: days before paying suppliers
  • Two cycles
    • Operating cycle = ICP + DSO
    • CCC = ICP + DSO − APP
  • Shorten the CCC → more FCF
    • Days cut × daily sales or COGS
    • Growth adds more next year

What the cycle is

Firms go round the same loop: buy inventory → sell finished goods → pay payables and collect receivables. The cash conversion cycle (CCC) measures, in days, how long the firm's own cash is tied up in that loop: from the day it pays its suppliers to the day it collects from customers.

CCC = Inventory Conversion Period + Receivables Collection Period − Payables Deferral Period = ICP (days in inventory) + DSO − APP Operating cycle (OC) = ICP + DSO ICP = Inventory / (COGS/365) DSO = A/R / (Sales/365) ← the only one that uses SALES APP = A/P / (COGS/365)

Your formula sheet writes these as CCC = ICP + RCP − PDP and OC = ICP + RCP. RCP = DSO = ACP, and PDP = APP. Same thing, different letters.

The slide 13 timeline

Inventory Pay Inventory Collect from purchased suppliers sold customers |--------------|---------------|------------------| |<---------- ICP (days in inventory) ->|<---- DSO ---->| |<-- APP --->|<------------------ CCC ----------------->| |<--------------------- Operating cycle --------------------->|
  • The operating cycle runs from buying inventory to collecting the cash: ICP + DSO.
  • Suppliers let you wait APP days before paying, so your cash is only tied up for the rest of it. That's why the CCC = operating cycle − APP.
  • Shorter CCC = less cash tied up = less financing needed = more FCF.

The cash conversion cycle counts the days your own cash is tied up: days in inventory plus days to collect, minus the days suppliers let you wait. Cutting days frees cash: multiply the days saved by one day of sales (for receivables) or one day of COGS (for inventory and payables).
